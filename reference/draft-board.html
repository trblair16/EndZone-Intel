Rules built from what actually happened to Team Nepotism in 2025 — not generic advice. Read these before you make an in-season move, not just on draft day.

1. Cap your transactions

You made 50 moves last season — most in the league by far — and still finished with the fewest total points scored, lower than the 2-11 team. Volume of moves didn't buy production. Set a soft personal limit (say, 2 moves a week) and make yourself justify going past it.

Evidence: season transaction log vs. points-for, all 8 teams.

2. Don't flip a starter off one outlier week

Three different QBs started in your first three weeks (Daniels → Fields → Goff), each swap chasing the previous week's box score. The same thing happened with Wilson/Odunze bouncing between bench and lineup. Week 4 — when Jones settled in and Wilson/Odunze both started together — was your tightest, most predictable week of the year. Stability outperformed chasing.

Evidence: Weeks 1-4 lineup data, QB and WR2/FLEX slots.

3. Watch for the bench-then-pop pattern

Your own read on last season: guys would underperform while started, you'd bench them, and the moment they came out they'd pop off. Before benching a struggling player, check whether the underlying role/target share is actually declining, or if it's just a quiet week. If the role hasn't changed, the points are more likely to still be coming.

Evidence: reported pattern, Wilson/Odunze usage weeks 1-4.

3b. A declining trend is not the same as one bad week

Etienne is the opposite case from Wilson/Odunze: 16.9 → 11.6 → 8.8 across three straight starts, each one worse than the last, with his best week of the stretch coming on your bench. That's a real trend, not a one-off dip. The "don't panic on one week" rule only applies to actual one-week outliers — if the score has fallen for 2-3 straight starts, that's a role change worth investigating, not noise to ride out.

Evidence: Etienne Weeks 1-5 proj/score.

4. Buy-low trades need proof, not projection

Egbuka cost a trade after Week 6 and gave nothing back; Collins got traded away right after a concussion, selling low on a healthy long-term asset. Before trading for or away a player mid-season, check the last 3 weeks of actual usage — not the name value or a single bad/good week.

Evidence: in-season trades for Egbuka, Judkins, Pittman, Hall; Collins trade-away.

5. Some injuries just aren't your fault

Hampton's best game of the season (27.5 pts) came the week before he landed on IR. Lamb, Bowers, and Daniels all lost time too. That's real bad luck stacked on a solid draft, not a scouting error — don't over-correct the whole strategy because of one unlucky season.

Evidence: Hampton Wk4 → Wk5 IR; Lamb/Bowers/Daniels missed time.

6. A declining trend while started is different from one bad week

Etienne's scores while started went 16.9 → 11.6 → 8.8 across three straight weeks, with his best game of the stretch coming in the one week he was benched. That's not a slump that snaps back — it's a role quietly shrinking start to start. If a player's production drops each consecutive week he's in your lineup, that's a trend to act on, not a one-off to ride out.

Evidence: Etienne weekly scores, Weeks 1-5.

7. A hot streak isn't a sample size yet

You picked up Juwan Johnson over Dallas Goedert because he'd looked like one of his QB's most trusted targets in a couple of recent games — then he busted the week you started him. The read on Goedert's risk (run-heavy offense, A.J. Brown soaking up targets) was actually correct reasoning. The mistake was treating 2-3 good games as equivalent information to an established, if capped, role. A short hot streak carries more downside than a known boom/bust floor, because you don't have enough data yet to know where the streak settles.

Evidence: Week 5 TE streaming decision, Juwan Johnson vs. Dallas Goedert.

8. A true 50/50 committee hurts both backs, not just one

Etienne's problem in Jacksonville was a single back's role quietly eroding while he stayed the nominal starter. Stevenson and Henderson in New England are a different failure mode entirely: neither back ever separated from the other, so both stayed too inconsistent to trust and too capped to have real upside — both spent most of last season on waivers despite real, non-trivial volume. When evaluating a "committee" flag, ask which version it is: a clear lead back with a handcuff (fine to draft the lead), or a genuine even split (risky for anyone involved, no matter who looks like the nominal starter on paper).

Evidence: Rhamondre Stevenson / TreVeyon Henderson 2025 season, New England backfield.

9. Don't stack two health-risk TEs — production risk is a different category

Two practice drafts landed on the same pattern by accident: both TE picks (Kittle + Kraft) were simultaneous health bets — real risk of missed games at the same position, with no fallback if either recovery ran slow. That's the specific thing to avoid. It's a different situation than pairing a health-risk TE with one who's simply inconsistent week to week (like Goedert, whose risk is TD/target variance, not availability) — that's one real bet plus one known, if boom-or-bust, floor, which is a defensible pairing. The rule is about not doubling up on "might not play," not about avoiding every TE with any flag next to his name.

Evidence: Practice Drafts 1 and 5 (Kittle + Kraft, both health risk) vs. Practice Draft 7 (Kittle + Goedert, one health risk + one production-variance).

Draft Shape: Round-by-Round Plan

Target end roster: ~5 RB, ~6 WR, 2 QB, 2 TE, 1 D/ST, 1 K (16 rounds, 9 starters + 7 bench).

R1–2: Best player available, RB or WR (elite tier) — a true bell-cow RB is the strongest injury insurance you can draft.
R3–4: Best of RB/WR, or an elite TE (Bowers/McBride-tier) if one of your targets falls — worth breaking pattern for, not worth reaching for.
R5–6: RB and WR depth — start building bench RB depth now, not in November like last year.
R7–8: QB and TE can both wait past this window — seven practice drafts confirm Stafford (plus a solid QB2 like Lawrence/Williams) and a real TE (Kittle/Kraft/Goedert/Ferguson-tier) are consistently still there at R9+. Exception: if Lamar Jackson is still on the board around R4–5, take him — his rushing floor is a genuinely scarce asset worth breaking the wait for.
R9–10: Lock QB1 and, if you didn't grab an elite TE early, TE1 here too — both positions have reliably deep value at this range, so this is the window to actually pull the trigger rather than wait further. Watch for: other managers reading the same market inefficiency and sniping Stafford or the Kittle/Kraft injury-discount tier earlier than raw ADP suggests — don't assume the value will still be sitting there without checking.
R11–13: RB depth, a handcuff for your own RB1, and final WR bench pieces.
R14: QB2 as a streamer/investment (Shough-tier) — insurance if your QB1 carries an injury flag.
R15: D/ST.
R16: K.
